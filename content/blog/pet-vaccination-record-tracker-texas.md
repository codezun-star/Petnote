There is a specific, avoidable moment that a lot of Texas pet owners run into: standing at a county window, or on the phone with a boarding kennel before a trip, being asked for a rabies certificate nobody can find. The vaccine was given. The clinic has moved, or changed practice management software, or the paper went into a drawer that has since been reorganised twice.

Texas is stricter than most states about connecting that certificate to everything else, which is exactly why it's worth having a system.

## What Texas actually requires

Under [Texas Health and Safety Code Section 826.021](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._health_&_safety_code_section_826.021), the owner of a dog or cat must have the animal vaccinated against rabies by the time it is four months old, and at regular intervals after that as prescribed by department rule.

Two details in that statute matter more than owners usually realise.

First, the veterinarian who administers the vaccine must issue you a vaccination certificate in a form meeting minimum standards approved by the state. The certificate is a document you are meant to hold, not just a note in the clinic's file.

Second, a county or municipality may not register or license an animal that has not been vaccinated in accordance with the section. Registration and vaccination are legally coupled. If you cannot produce the certificate, the tag does not get issued — not because the clerk is being difficult, but because the statute does not permit it.

Failing to vaccinate when required is a Class C misdemeanor, and a Class B misdemeanor if the court finds a prior conviction.

### Cats are not an afterthought

The state statute names dogs *and* cats. Owners of indoor cats often assume the requirement is aimed at dogs that go outside. It isn't written that way, and many Texas counties and cities reinforce the cat requirement through their own ordinances. If you have a cat, you need the certificate too.

## Why Texas records are worth more than a rabies date

Rabies compliance is the legal floor. The medical reason to keep a fuller record in Texas is parasite pressure.

Texas has been reported at the top of national canine heartworm incidence, with the heaviest concentration in the eastern part of the state, according to [AVMA coverage of the American Heartworm Society's survey](https://www.avma.org/news/texas-tops-nation-heartworm-cases-parasite-also-detected-unexpected-regions). Heartworm is not a one-appointment problem. It involves an annual test, a prevention product given on a schedule, and — if a dog does test positive — a treatment course that runs for months and needs to be documented.

That is a lot of dates. And unlike a rabies booster, nobody sends you a legal reminder about any of it.

The practical consequence is that a Texas dog's useful record has two layers: the certificate that satisfies the county, and the running history that tells a veterinarian what has been given, when the last test was run, and whether prevention actually stayed continuous through a hot summer when the tube of medication sat unopened on a shelf.

## Building a record that survives a move

Texas is a state people move around in. Between Houston, Dallas–Fort Worth, Austin and San Antonio, changing counties often means changing veterinary practices, and that is where histories break.

A record worth having answers four questions without a phone call:

- **When was the rabies vaccine given, and by whom?** With the certificate itself attached, not just a remembered date.
- **When is the next one due?** Which depends on whether a one-year or three-year product was used.
- **What is the heartworm status?** Last test date, result, and what prevention is being given.
- **What else is on file?** Prior surgeries, chronic conditions, allergies, current medications.

The first two are compliance. The last two are what a new veterinarian in a new city actually needs on the first visit, and what you will not be able to reconstruct from memory two years later.

### A note on where the paper lives

Clinics keep records, but they keep *their* records. A practice that closes, sells, or migrates systems is not obliged to hand you a decade of history in a usable form, and a Saturday emergency clinic cannot see your regular vet's files at all. The copy you control is the one that is there when it matters.

## Start with the certificate you already have

If you do one thing after reading this, photograph the rabies certificate currently sitting in your file and store it somewhere you can find from a phone. That single document unlocks county registration, most boarding kennels, most groomers, and any emergency clinic that needs to know your dog is covered.

Then add the heartworm test date. In Texas, that is the second most useful number in the file.
