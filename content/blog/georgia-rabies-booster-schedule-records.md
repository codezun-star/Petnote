There is a specific misunderstanding that costs Georgia pet owners money and, occasionally, compliance. It goes like this: *the vet gave her the three-year shot, so we're done until 2029.*

In Georgia, that is wrong at least once, and in some counties it is wrong every year.

## The rule that overrides the label

Georgia's rabies control framework sits under [O.C.G.A. Title 31, Chapter 19](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-31/chapter-19/section-31-19-5/) and is administered through the [Georgia Department of Public Health's rabies manual](https://dph.georgia.gov/document/publication/georgia-rabies-manual-updated-april-2018/download). Owned dogs and cats must be vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian, using approved vaccines, in accordance with the national Compendium of Animal Rabies Prevention and Control.

Here is the part people miss. A veterinarian has discretion to administer either a one-year or a three-year labelled rabies vaccine as the **initial** dose. But revaccination is required **one year following the initial dose** — regardless of the animal's age, and regardless of which vaccine was used as that first dose.

So the three-year label does not buy three years the first time. It buys one. Only after that booster does the product label govern: from then on, how long an animal counts as "currently vaccinated" is determined strictly by the label of the last vaccine administered.

This means the answer to "when is my dog next due?" cannot be derived from the dog's age or from a general rule. It depends on which dose this was, and which product was used. Two facts that live on a certificate.

### And then there are one-year counties

Georgia layers a local variable on top. In counties designated as "one-year" counties for the purposes of revaccination, revaccination is required annually. A one-year or a three-year labelled vaccine may be administered, but the interval is yearly regardless.

An owner who moves from a three-year county to a one-year county, carrying a certificate that says the vaccine is good until 2029, is now on a different schedule than the certificate implies.

## Twelve weeks, and only from a veterinarian

Two further specifics worth knowing:

Dogs, cats and ferrets may receive rabies vaccination at **twelve weeks** of age or older under Georgia guidance — earlier than the four-month threshold several neighbouring states use.

And rabies vaccinations are recognised in Georgia **only when performed by a licensed veterinarian**. A vaccine administered elsewhere, or by someone else, may not count for Georgia purposes. If you have moved into Georgia with an animal vaccinated in a state that permits certified lay vaccinators, this is worth confirming rather than assuming.

## What this means you have to write down

Most states let you get away with recording a single fact: the date of the last rabies shot. Georgia effectively requires three:

1. **The date it was given.**
2. **Whether it was the initial dose or a booster** — because the initial dose triggers the one-year rule.
3. **Whether the product was one-year or three-year labelled** — because after the first booster, that label sets the interval.

Miss any one of those and you cannot compute the next due date. You can only guess, or call the clinic.

That is a genuinely unusual burden, and it is the reason Georgia owners benefit from writing things down more than owners in states with a flat annual rule.

It also explains a frustration Georgia owners report often: two veterinary practices giving apparently contradictory answers about when a dog is next due. Usually neither is wrong. One is reading the label, the other is applying the one-year booster rule or a county designation, and the disagreement dissolves the moment somebody produces the certificate showing which dose this was.

## Warm, humid, and full of parasites

Georgia's other record-keeping pressure is climate. The state appears among those with the highest heartworm case counts nationally in American Heartworm Society reporting, alongside its neighbours across the Southeast.

Heartworm prevention is a monthly commitment, tested annually. Like the rabies schedule, it is a sequence rather than an event, and it degrades quietly: a missed month in August does not announce itself, and by the following spring nobody remembers it happened.

Logging the dose alongside the vaccination dates puts the whole preventive picture in one place, which is what a veterinarian is actually asking about when they say "is he up to date?"
