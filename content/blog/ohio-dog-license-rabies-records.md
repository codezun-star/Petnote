Ohio is a useful state to write about precisely because it breaks the pattern. Most articles about pet paperwork start with "your state requires..." Ohio's answer is: it depends which county you are standing in.

## The statewide gap

Ohio does not carry a general statewide statute compelling rabies vaccination for pets the way Texas, Florida or New York do. What Ohio has instead is a licensing system, and a delegation of authority to local government.

[Ohio Revised Code Section 955.26](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-955.26) addresses the connection: proof of rabies vaccination within a satisfactory period must be demonstrated to the county auditor before a registration is issued for any dog **that is required to be vaccinated**.

Read that last clause carefully. The proof requirement attaches to dogs that are required to be vaccinated — and whether yours is depends on local regulation.

Local governments in Ohio are authorised to enact rabies vaccination regulations, and many have done so. In parts of northeastern Ohio, dogs and cats over sixteen weeks are required to be vaccinated. Elsewhere, the county's own licensing rules do the work: Franklin County, for instance, requires all dogs four months or older to have a current rabies vaccination before a licence is issued.

The practical upshot for an owner is unhelpful as a general rule and very simple in a specific case: **find out what your county requires, once, and then act as though it is the law — because for you, it is.**

## Licensing is the part with a deadline

[Ohio Revised Code Section 955.02](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-955.02) and the county auditors that administer it set a rhythm most owners only half-remember.

Dogs must be licensed in the county of residence. Renewals run in a window — December 1 through January 20 — rather than on the anniversary of whenever you first registered. New licences must be obtained within 30 days of arriving in a county, or when a puppy reaches three months.

A renewal window in the second half of December is, to put it gently, badly timed against the rest of a household's December. It is the single easiest deadline in Ohio pet ownership to sail past.

## What "it depends on your county" means for record keeping

When rules are local, your records have to be more portable than the rules are.

Ohio has substantial movement between counties — Franklin, Cuyahoga, Hamilton, Summit and their surrounding suburbs — and someone moving from a county with no vaccination requirement to one with a strict one does not get a grace period based on ignorance. They get 30 days to license, and the licence requires proof.

The person who has kept a dated record can comply in an afternoon. The person relying on a clinic across the state finds out how long records requests take.

### The four things worth having

- **The rabies certificate**, with the date and issuing veterinarian, whether or not your current county demands it. Counties change; certificates do not.
- **The current licence number and county.** This resets when you move.
- **Whether the last vaccine was a one-year or three-year product**, because that determines when "current" stops being true.
- **The rest of the medical history**, which no county asks for and every new veterinarian does.

## Four seasons, four different risks

Ohio's climate gives a dog a genuinely varied year — humid summers with mosquito activity, real winters, and long shoulder seasons. That variety is an argument for logging preventive care rather than trusting recall, because the products change with the season and the gaps open when the weather turns.

Heartworm has been reported in parts of the Midwest at higher densities than owners tend to assume, including hot spots in southern Illinois just across the state line. "We're too far north for that" is a belief worth checking with your veterinarian rather than holding by default.

## Somewhere the county cannot lose it

The county auditor keeps a record of your licence. Your veterinarian keeps a record of the vaccine. Neither of them keeps a record of your dog — the whole animal, across practices and counties and years.
