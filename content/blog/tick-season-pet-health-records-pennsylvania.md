Two things about Pennsylvania make a dog's health record more than an administrative nicety. One is a tick. The other is a 48-hour deadline written into state regulation.

## The 48-hour clause

Most states require rabies vaccination. Pennsylvania adds a specific obligation about proof.

Under the Commonwealth's rabies regulations at [7 Pa. Code Chapter 16](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F007%2Fchapter16%2Fchap16toc.html), a person owning or keeping a dog or cat over three months of age must, upon request of a police officer, State dog warden or designated municipal animal control officer, **produce within 48 hours a valid certificate of vaccination**.

That is a different kind of requirement from "have the animal vaccinated." It presumes you can lay hands on a document within two days of being asked. If the certificate is in a folder at a practice you left three years ago, you have a problem that a phone call may or may not solve inside the window.

The underlying requirement: a dog or cat over three months of age must be vaccinated with a USDA-licensed vaccine — including products producing three-year immunity — in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, administered only by or under the supervision of a licensed veterinarian.

And the booster timing is prescriptive: unless the manufacturer's directions indicate otherwise, a booster must be administered **between 12 and 14 months** from the date of the initial vaccination, regardless of the animal's age at first vaccination. That is a two-month window, not an open-ended year.

## The tick problem

Pennsylvania has for years been at or near the top of national Lyme disease reporting. State and news reporting put the Commonwealth at [16,624 laboratory-confirmed human cases in 2024](https://www.yourerie.com/news/local-news/pennsylvania-among-the-highest-states-for-lyme-disease-cases-experts-say-why/), and the CDC has long cautioned that confirmed case counts substantially understate true infection numbers.

For dogs, the [Pennsylvania Game Commission](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/pgc/wildlife/wildlife-health/wildlife-diseases/lyme-disease) has described canine Lyme incidence in the Commonwealth as varying between roughly 10 and 13 percent.

The vector is the blacklegged tick, *Ixodes scapularis*. The reasons Pennsylvania is such good habitat are structural rather than seasonal: abundant deer and small-mammal hosts, fragmented forest that creates edge habitat, suburban development pushing housing into that edge, and warmer seasons that lengthen the window when ticks are active.

### Why this becomes a records problem

Tick-borne disease is diagnosed against a timeline. A veterinarian assessing a limping, lethargic dog wants to know when the last tick preventive was actually given, whether the dog has been tested before, what the result was, and whether anything has changed since.

"Sometime in the spring" is not an answer that helps. And because tick prevention is usually a monthly product bought a few months at a time, the gaps are real: a box runs out in July, the reorder happens in September, and nobody writes down that August happened without cover.

A dated log of what was given turns a vague history into a usable one. It also stops the opposite error — assuming a dog is protected when the last dose was three months ago.

## What Pennsylvania owners should keep

- **The rabies certificate itself.** Because of the 48-hour rule, this is the one document where "I know he's vaccinated" is not sufficient.
- **The booster date**, with the 12-to-14-month window in mind for the first one.
- **Tick preventive doses**, with dates. Monthly products are the easiest thing in veterinary care to lose track of.
- **Any tick-borne disease testing** and its result, so a future test has something to compare against.
- **Weight**, tracked over time. Dosing for many preventives is weight-banded, and a growing dog crosses bands.

Pennsylvania's geography also spreads the risk unevenly. A dog walked on suburban lawns outside Philadelphia and a dog worked over farmland in the north-central counties are meeting different tick populations in different densities, and what your veterinarian recommends will reflect that. Recording what was actually given, rather than what is generally sensible, is what makes the history useful to a practice you have not visited before.

## The seasonal trap

Pennsylvania has real winters, and it is tempting to treat tick prevention as a summer activity. Milder shoulder seasons have extended tick activity, which is precisely the mechanism researchers point to when explaining rising case counts. Whether your dog needs year-round cover is a conversation for your veterinarian, who knows your county and your dog's exposure — but it should be a conversation, not an assumption made by whoever stops buying the product in October.
