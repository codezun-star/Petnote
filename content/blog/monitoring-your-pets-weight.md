If you only track one number about your pet, track their weight. It's cheap to measure, it needs no equipment beyond a scale, and it moves before most other signs do. Weight change is one of the earliest measurable signals that something is wrong — often weeks or months before an owner notices a behavioural change.

The catch is that it's only useful as a **trend**. A single number tells you almost nothing.

> This is general guidance, not veterinary advice. Any weight change you're unsure about is worth a phone call to your vet.

## Why the trend matters more than the number

"Is 18 kg healthy for a Border Collie?" is close to unanswerable. Healthy ranges vary by breed, frame, age and sex, and the ranges themselves are wide.

"Has this Border Collie lost 2 kg over four months without a change in diet?" is very answerable. That's a red flag, and you don't need to know the breed standard to see it.

This is the core idea: **your pet is their own baseline**. Once you have a handful of entries, deviation from their own normal is far more informative than comparison to a chart.

## The percentage rule of thumb

Because pets vary so much in size, changes are best read as percentages of body weight rather than absolute amounts.

A commonly used threshold in small-animal practice is that **an unexplained loss of around 5% of body weight is worth investigating, and 10% is significant.**

| Pet | 5% of body weight | 10% of body weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cat, 4.5 kg | 225 g | 450 g |
| Small dog, 8 kg | 400 g | 800 g |
| Medium dog, 20 kg | 1 kg | 2 kg |
| Large dog, 35 kg | 1.75 kg | 3.5 kg |

Look at the cat row. **225 grams.** That is genuinely invisible by eye and by feel — you will not notice it while stroking your cat, and you will not notice it in a photo. It is trivially visible on a chart. This is the entire argument for writing the numbers down.

The word doing the work in that threshold is *unexplained*. Weight coming off during a deliberate, vet-supervised diet is the plan working. The concern is weight moving when nothing about the pet's routine has changed.

## How to weigh consistently

Inconsistent weighing produces noise that looks like signal. Four rules:

**Same scale every time.** Two scales can disagree by several hundred grams, which for a cat is the entire threshold you're watching for. If you weigh at the clinic, use the clinic scale each time.

**Same time of day, ideally before a meal.** A large dog can swing over a kilogram across a day depending on food and water.

**For small pets, weigh yourself, then yourself holding the pet, and subtract.** Household bathroom scales are imprecise at low weights. For cats and small dogs, a kitchen scale under a carrier is more accurate — just remember to subtract the carrier.

**Record it immediately.** A number you plan to write down later is a number you won't write down.

## How often

- **Puppies and kittens:** weekly. They should be gaining steadily, and a plateau is worth asking about.
- **Healthy adults:** monthly, or at minimum at every vet visit.
- **Seniors (roughly 7+ for dogs, 10+ for cats):** monthly at least. Gradual weight loss is common in older animals and easy to write off as "just ageing" when it's often treatable.
- **Any pet on a weight management plan or with a chronic condition:** whatever interval your vet asks for, usually weekly or fortnightly.

## Pair the number with a body condition check

Weight alone misses changes in body composition — an animal can lose muscle and gain fat at a stable weight. A 30-second hands-on check catches that:

- **Ribs.** You should feel them easily under a thin layer of fat, without pressing hard. Ribs you can see clearly at rest suggest underweight; ribs you can't find suggest overweight.
- **Waist from above.** There should be a visible narrowing behind the ribs.
- **Profile from the side.** The belly should tuck up toward the hind legs rather than running straight or sagging.

Do this at the same time you weigh. Two data points, thirty seconds.

## When to call the vet

Contact your vet if you see:

- Unexplained loss of **5% or more** of body weight
- Steady loss across **three or more consecutive** measurements
- Rapid gain without a change in food, especially with a swollen abdomen
- Weight loss alongside increased thirst, increased appetite, vomiting, or lethargy
- Any loss at all in a senior pet, however small
- A young animal that has stopped gaining

**Weight loss with a normal or increased appetite is particularly worth acting on quickly.** In cats it's a classic presentation for hyperthyroidism and diabetes; in dogs it can point to malabsorption or other conditions. Both are far more manageable when caught early.

- Track the **trend**, not a single number — your pet is their own baseline.
- Roughly **5% unexplained loss warrants a call; 10% is significant**.
