The moment you realize your pet isn't where they should be, your brain stops working properly. That's normal, and it's exactly why a checklist helps — someone has already done the thinking, and you just have to follow it.

Read this now, while nothing is wrong.

## The first hour

**1. Search the immediate area first, and search low.**
Frightened animals don't run for miles. They hide, and they hide in places that seem far too small. Under the deck, behind the shed, inside the garage you're sure was closed, up a tree, under a parked car. Cats in particular tend to stay within a few houses of home for the first day or two.

**2. Take their food, their carrier, and something that smells like home.**
Shake the treat bag. Open a tin. For cats, putting their litter tray and bedding outside the door gives them a scent trail to follow back.

**3. Call, then be quiet and listen.**
Call their name, then stop completely for thirty seconds. An injured or trapped animal may only manage a very faint sound, and you will not hear it over your own voice.

**4. Search at dusk and at night.**
Counter-intuitive, but it works. Streets are quieter, so a hiding animal is more likely to move — and a torch will catch the reflection of their eyes from a long way off.

**5. Tell the neighbours immediately.**
Ask them specifically to check garages, sheds, greenhouses and cars. A staggering number of missing cats are found locked in a neighbour's outbuilding.

## The first day

**Call the microchip registry** and report your pet as missing. This is the step most people skip. A microchip only works if the registry has current contact details — and if the number isn't flagged as missing, a scan at a shelter may not raise any alarm.

**Contact, in this order:**

- Every veterinary clinic within about 10 km, including emergency clinics
- Local shelters and rescue organizations — **visit in person if you can**, because staff descriptions and your idea of "brown tabby" may not match
- The local council or animal control
- Any road maintenance service that handles animal collisions

**Post online, with a good photo.** Local community groups, neighbourhood apps and lost-pet pages. Include:

- A clear, recent photo showing distinguishing marks
- Where and when they were last seen
- Species, breed, size, colour, collar description
- **A phone number that you will actually answer**
- Whether the animal is nervous, needs medication, or should not be chased

That last point matters more than people expect. A well-meaning stranger chasing a frightened dog can push them several kilometres further away.

**Be careful about mentioning a reward.** It attracts scam calls. If you do offer one, don't publish a specific amount.

## Physical posters still work

Digital reaches people who follow the right pages. Posters reach the person walking their own dog past your pet's hiding place.

Design them to be read from a moving car: very large photo, the single word **LOST** at the top, a phone number in the largest text that fits, and minimal other detail. Put them at eye level at junctions, outside shops, at park entrances and at bus stops. Waterproof them.

## The first week

Keep going. Animals are recovered days and sometimes weeks later — hunger eventually overcomes fear, and a cat that was hiding silently on day two will often start calling on day four.

Re-check the shelters every couple of days rather than relying on a single call. Intake volumes are high, and a description that didn't match on Monday might match a new arrival on Wednesday.

## The preparation that changes the outcome

Everything above is reactive. Here's the proactive part.

The single biggest bottleneck in getting a pet home is not whether someone finds them — it's **how quickly the person who finds them can reach you**. A microchip is excellent, but it requires the finder to take the animal to a vet or shelter with a scanner. That can take hours, or a day, or may not happen at all if the person assumes the animal is just wandering.

A visible tag closes that gap. And a tag with a QR code closes it further: the finder points their phone camera at it and gets your number immediately, without needing an app or an account.

- **Now:** microchip registered with current details, visible tag, QR tag, recent photo saved somewhere you can find it fast
- **First hour:** search close and low, treats and carrier, call-then-listen, alert neighbours
- **First day:** flag the microchip as missing, call vets and shelters, post online with a clear photo
