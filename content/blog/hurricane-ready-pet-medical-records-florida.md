Storm season reorganises your priorities in about ninety seconds. You are thinking about the car, the route, the crate, whether the cat will go into the carrier. You are not thinking about a vaccination certificate — right up until someone at an intake table asks for one.

That is the specific gap this article is about.

## The year-round requirement

Start with what Florida asks of you regardless of the weather.

[Florida Statute 828.30](https://www.flsenate.gov/laws/statutes/2025/828.30) requires all dogs, cats and ferrets four months of age or older to be vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian, with a vaccine licensed by the USDA for that species.

The revaccination rule is the part that trips people up. The statute requires the animal to be revaccinated **12 months after the initial vaccination**. Only after that does the interval follow the manufacturer's directions — which may be one year or three, depending on the product used.

So a kitten vaccinated at four months is due again at sixteen months, even if the vaccine carried a three-year label. Owners who read "three-year vaccine" and mentally file the problem away for three years are, in that first cycle, wrong.

There is a medical exemption: a licensed veterinarian who examines the animal and certifies in writing that vaccination would endanger its health because of age, infirmity, disability, illness or other medical considerations.

Ferrets are named explicitly in the Florida statute, which is not true everywhere. If you keep one, the requirement is yours too.

## What changes when a storm is coming

The federal [PETS Act](https://aldf.org/article/the-pets-act-companion-animals-affected-by-natural-disasters/), passed in 2006 after Hurricane Katrina, requires states to account for households with pets and service animals in their emergency preparedness plans in order to receive federal funding for those plans. That is why pet-friendly public sheltering exists at all in most Florida counties.

What it does not do is make intake informal. Counties operating pet-friendly shelters generally run them with strict intake rules for health and safety, separate areas for animals and people, limited space, and a request for vaccination records for each pet. Requirements are set locally, so the specifics differ between, say, Miami-Dade and Monroe County — and the time to read your county's page is now, not while the bridge is closing.

Boarding facilities and veterinary practices taking in displaced animals ask for the same thing, often for a longer list of vaccines than the law itself requires.

### Why "it's at the vet's office" is not an answer

During an evacuation, your regular clinic may be closed, may be in the evacuation zone itself, or may be unreachable because everyone else is calling too. A record that requires a phone call to a specific building during a regional emergency is not a record you have.

## The file worth assembling in August

Not an exhaustive archive. Five things, reachable from a phone:

1. **Rabies certificate for each animal**, with the date and the issuing veterinarian.
2. **Other vaccinations on file.** Shelters and boarding facilities frequently ask about distemper and parvovirus for dogs, and panleukopenia for cats, beyond the rabies requirement in statute.
3. **Current medications with dosages.** If your dog takes something daily, the pharmacy you end up near will need the name and the dose, not a description of the pill's colour.
4. **A recent photograph of you with the animal.** This does double duty: a lost-pet notice, and proof of ownership if you are separated and reunited through a shelter.
5. **Allergies and chronic conditions**, written down rather than remembered under stress.

## Humidity is a year-round problem too

Florida's other record-keeping argument is parasites. Heartworm hot spots reported in the Florida Panhandle and southwest Florida mean prevention here is not seasonal in the way it is further north, and a lapse of a couple of months is a genuine exposure rather than a scheduling inconvenience.

That makes the prevention log — what was given, and when — part of the medical history rather than a nicety. A dog that missed two doses in September is a different patient from one that did not, and eighteen months later nobody remembers which is which.
