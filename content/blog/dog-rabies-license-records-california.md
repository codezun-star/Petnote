Most guides to California pet paperwork make the same mistake: they treat vaccination and licensing as one task with one deadline. The statute treats them as two, with different trigger ages, and the gap between them is where owners fall out of compliance without noticing.

## Two clocks, not one

[California Health and Safety Code Section 121690](https://california.public.law/codes/health_and_safety_code_section_121690) sets them out separately.

The vaccination clock starts at **three months**. Once a dog reaches that age, the owner must have it vaccinated by a licensed veterinarian with an approved canine antirabies vaccine, administered according to the vaccine label. There is an exemption where a licensed veterinarian determines that vaccination would endanger the dog's life because of disease or other considerations.

The licensing clock starts at **four months**. After that age, the owner must secure a licence as provided by the ordinance of the responsible city or county, no less than once every two years. And the statute adds a deadline on top: the vaccination and the licence must both be procured no later than 30 days after the dog turns four months old.

So a puppy that gets its rabies vaccine at three months and a licence at four months and one week is fine. A puppy whose owner waits until five months has missed a statutory window, even if the vaccine itself was eventually given.

### The exemption is not a loophole

Where a dog is exempt from vaccination on veterinary grounds, the local health officer may require it to be confined to the owner's premises, or leashed on a lead not exceeding six feet under the direct physical control of an adult when off the premises. An exemption changes what you must do, not whether the state is paying attention.

## Why the licence renewal is the part people miss

A rabies vaccine on a three-year product feels like a solved problem. The licence, renewable at least every two years under local ordinance, runs on a different cycle — and the renewal notice goes to whatever address the county had on file when you last registered.

California is a state with a lot of internal movement. People change apartments, change counties, and change veterinary practices. The renewal notice follows the old address. The vaccine record stays with the old clinic. Neither failure announces itself until you need the paperwork.

## Records in wildfire country

There is a second reason Californians benefit from a portable record, and it has nothing to do with compliance.

Evacuations happen with very little notice, and they happen to households that had no reason to think about paperwork that morning. When you leave with a carrier and a leash, the things that turn out to matter are the things already on your phone: who your dog is, what they take, what they react to, and how to reach you.

A boarding facility taking in displaced animals will ask about vaccination status. A veterinarian who has never seen your dog will ask what medications they are on. A neighbour who ends up with your cat will need your number. None of that is retrievable from a filing cabinet in a house you cannot return to.

This is the argument for keeping the record somewhere that is not the house. Not because a fire is likely, but because the cost of preparing is a few minutes and the cost of not preparing lands on the worst day of the year.

## What a complete California file looks like

- **The rabies certificate**, with the date given and whether the product was a one-year or three-year label. The label determines when the next one is due; the certificate is what the county wants to see.
- **The current licence number and its expiry**, plus which jurisdiction issued it. This is the piece that moves when you move.
- **Chronic conditions and current medications.** The information an unfamiliar veterinarian needs first.
- **Allergies**, stated plainly. This is the single most useful line in an emergency.
- **A recent photograph.** Useful for a lost pet notice, and useful for proving ownership at an evacuation shelter.
