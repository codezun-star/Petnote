Vaccination schedules sound simple until you're standing in the clinic trying to remember whether the last booster was fourteen or twenty-six months ago. This guide covers what the schedule actually looks like, why the intervals are what they are, and how to keep track without relying on memory.

> This is general guidance, not veterinary advice. Vaccination requirements vary by country, region and individual animal. Your vet's recommendation always wins.

## Core vs non-core vaccines

Every vaccination schedule splits into two groups.

**Core vaccines** are recommended for essentially every animal of that species, regardless of where they live or how they spend their days. They protect against diseases that are widespread, severe, or transmissible to humans.

**Non-core vaccines** depend on lifestyle and geography. A dog that boards at a kennel twice a year has different risks than one that never leaves a suburban garden.

| Species | Typical core vaccines | Common non-core vaccines |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dog | Distemper, parvovirus, adenovirus, rabies | Leptospirosis, Bordetella, Lyme, canine influenza |
| Cat | Panleukopenia, calicivirus, herpesvirus, rabies | Feline leukemia, chlamydia, Bordetella |

Rabies is a special case: in many jurisdictions it's a legal requirement rather than a recommendation, and the interval is set by law, not by the vaccine's actual duration of immunity.

## The puppy and kitten series

Young animals get a series of shots rather than a single dose, and the reason is worth understanding because it explains why the timing is strict.

Newborns absorb antibodies from their mother's first milk. Those maternal antibodies protect them for the first weeks of life — but they also **interfere with vaccines**. A vaccine given while maternal antibodies are still high gets neutralized before it can teach the immune system anything.

The problem is that nobody knows exactly when a given animal's maternal antibodies fade. It varies. So the series exists to cover the uncertainty:

1. **6–8 weeks** — first dose
2. **10–12 weeks** — second dose
3. **14–16 weeks** — third dose
4. **12 months** — first booster

Each dose is a fresh attempt. Whichever one lands after the maternal antibodies have dropped is the one that actually works.

**This is why the final dose matters most.** A puppy that gets doses one and two but misses the 16-week dose may have no meaningful protection at all — and this is the single most common gap we see in the records people import into Petnote. Until roughly a week after the final dose, a young animal should stay away from dog parks, communal water bowls and unknown animals.

## Adult boosters: less often than you'd think

The old advice was annual boosters for everything. Current guidance from most veterinary bodies is more nuanced: **core vaccines for adults are typically boosted every three years**, not annually, because immunity from modern core vaccines lasts considerably longer than a year.

Non-core vaccines are different. Leptospirosis and Bordetella immunity fades faster, so those genuinely are annual — sometimes more often for high-exposure animals.

The practical consequence: your pet's schedule is not one number. Some items are due yearly, some every three years, and rabies runs on whatever cycle your local law specifies. Three separate clocks, none of them aligned.

## What actually goes wrong

In practice, missed vaccines almost never come from someone deciding to skip one. They come from:

- **The three-year interval.** Twelve months is easy to remember. Thirty-six is not.
- **Changing vets.** Records don't always follow, and the new clinic starts from what you can tell them.
- **The paper card.** It lives in a drawer, and it isn't in the drawer when you need it.
- **Multiple pets on different cycles.** Two animals, six vaccines, staggered dates.

None of these are memory failures exactly. They're record-keeping failures.

## Keeping the schedule without thinking about it

A few habits that hold up:

- **Record the next due date at the moment of the appointment**, not later. The vet just told you; write it down before you leave the room.
- **Note who administered it.** If you switch clinics, the new vet may want to confirm what was given.
- **Keep the certificate.** Boarding kennels, groomers and international travel all ask for proof, usually with less notice than you'd like.
- **Set the reminder further out than the due date.** A week's warning gives you time to actually get an appointment.

## The short version

- Core vaccines apply to everyone; non-core depend on lifestyle and location.
- The puppy and kitten series exists because maternal antibodies block vaccines — **the final dose around 16 weeks is the one that counts**.
- Adult core boosters are typically every three years; several non-core vaccines are annual.
- The hard part isn't the medicine, it's remembering three different intervals across however many animals you have.
