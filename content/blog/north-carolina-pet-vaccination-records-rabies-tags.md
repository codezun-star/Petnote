A rabies tag is a small aluminium disc with a number stamped on it. In North Carolina it is also, legally speaking, something your dog is supposed to be wearing right now.

That requirement makes North Carolina worth understanding in a bit more detail than most states, because the tag creates an illusion of completeness. The tag proves a vaccine happened. It tells you almost nothing else.

## The statute

[G.S. 130A-185](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/PDF/BySection/Chapter_130A/GS_130A-185.pdf) requires the owner of a dog, cat or ferret over four months of age to have the animal vaccinated against rabies. Only animal rabies vaccine licensed by the USDA and approved by the Commission may be used on animals in the state.

North Carolina is more permissive than most states about who may administer it. A rabies vaccine may be given by a licensed veterinarian, by a registered veterinary technician under the direct supervision of a licensed veterinarian, **or by a certified rabies vaccinator**. That last category is why county rabies clinics in North Carolina can run at the scale and price they do.

The enforcement side has teeth. As [UNC's School of Government has summarised](https://canons.sog.unc.edu/blog/2017/07/21/significant-change-north-carolinas-rabies-law-2/), an owner who fails to comply may be charged with a Class 1 misdemeanour, or the local health director may seek an injunction.

## The tag rule, specifically

Dogs must wear rabies tags **at all times**, and may be impounded if found at large without one.

State law extends the tag requirement to cats and ferrets as well, though a local government may adopt an ordinance exempting them — which many do, since a collar on an outdoor cat is a different risk calculation than a collar on a dog.

For dog owners the practical reading is straightforward: the tag is not for the filing cabinet. It goes on the collar, and it stays there.

## Why the tag is not the record

Here is where owners get caught out.

The tag carries a number. It does not carry the date the vaccine was given, the product used, or when the next dose is due. If your dog is picked up, the tag lets animal control trace the vaccination back through the issuing authority. If *you* need to know whether your dog is current — before a boarding stay, a grooming appointment, or a move out of state — the tag is useless.

Worse, tags fall off. A tag lost in a creek in June does not un-vaccinate your dog, but it does remove the only physical evidence most owners carry.

The certificate is the record. The tag is a pointer to it.

## North Carolina's other reason to keep a history

The state sits inside the region with the heaviest reported canine heartworm burden in the country, and the central Carolinas have been identified in American Heartworm Society reporting as a hot spot where case density is markedly higher than surrounding areas.

That has a direct consequence for how a North Carolina dog's record should look. Alongside the rabies date, the file wants:

- **The last heartworm test date and result.** Testing is annual for a reason; a result from two years ago is not a current fact.
- **The prevention product and the months it was actually given.** Not "we give it year-round," but the dates.
- **Weight over time**, because most preventives are dosed in weight bands and a dog that crosses a band on an unchanged dose is under-dosed.

## Coast, piedmont, mountains

North Carolina's geography spans a genuinely wide range of conditions, and a dog in Asheville does not face the same year as a dog in Wilmington. Mosquito season length differs. Tick species differ. What your veterinarian recommends will differ accordingly, which is another argument for a record that shows what was actually done rather than what is generally advisable.

If you move within the state — and people do, frequently, between the Triangle, Charlotte and the coast — that history is the thing a new practice will want and the thing that is hardest to reconstruct.
